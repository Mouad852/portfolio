## Try it before you read this

**[myheart.mdchaouni.workers.dev](https://myheart.mdchaouni.workers.dev)** — the demo runs on
in-memory fixtures, but it reproduces the parts that matter faithfully: authorization, record
ownership, the appointment state machine, and writes that persist for your session. Sign in as a
doctor and then as a receptionist; you are looking at two genuinely different applications.

There is also a [full walkthrough on video](https://youtu.be/AJEzYZvm-dY), and the
[source is on GitHub](https://github.com/Mouad852/MedCore).

## The problem

A clinic is not one application. A receptionist books and reschedules but must never see a
diagnosis. A doctor writes prescriptions but has no business inside the billing ledger. A lab
technician uploads results for requests assigned to them and nothing else. A patient sees exactly
their own record and no one else's.

Most systems model this as a single `role` column and a pile of `if` statements in the UI. That
fails in the way security usually fails — quietly, and only for the person who finds the gap.

## What I built

Six domain microservices — patients, appointments, billing, prescriptions, lab, and users — behind
a **Spring Cloud Gateway**, with Eureka for service discovery, OpenFeign for service-to-service
calls, and Resilience4j circuit breakers so one slow service cannot take the rest down. Each
service owns its own PostgreSQL database; nothing reaches across a schema boundary.

Sixteen containers in total: eight services, seven databases, and Keycloak.

### Authorization in three layers

This is the part I care about, and the reason the project exists.

**At the gateway**, role rules reject requests that have no business reaching a service at all — a
receptionist's token never gets near the prescription API.

**Inside each service**, the JWT is validated independently. A service does not trust that
something upstream already checked; a request arriving by any other path is treated the same way.

**At the record**, ownership is checked against the token's claims. Passing the first two layers
gets you to the right endpoint — it does not get you someone else's chart. A doctor requesting a
patient they are not assigned to is refused at the row, not at the route.

Authentication runs on **Keycloak** with OpenID Connect, using the Authorization Code flow with
PKCE. The defaults are deliberately fail-closed: an unclear request is denied, not allowed.

### The frontend

A **React** interface tailored to seven roles, with TanStack Query managing server state. Each role
gets a different navigation, a different dashboard, and different affordances — not the same screen
with buttons hidden by CSS. The hosted demo reproduces all of it without a backend, which is what
makes the link at the top of this page worth clicking.

## Results

- **6 domain microservices**, 16 containers, database-per-service
- **7 roles**, each with a genuinely distinct interface
- **149 tests** — 92 Vitest on the frontend, 57 JUnit across three backend modules, covering
  appointment lifecycle, booking rules, access control and payment status
- A **live demo** anyone can open without installing anything

## What I would do differently

The three-layer model is right, but the gateway rules and the per-service rules are maintained
separately, so adding a role means editing in two places. A shared policy service — the Cerbos
approach I later used at SYNERTIC — would make the rules one artifact instead of two, and would
make them testable on their own.
